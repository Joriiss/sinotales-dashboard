The Giant Wild Goose Pagoda, also known as the Big Wild Goose Pagoda (Dayan Pagoda), is a monumental Buddhist pagoda located in southern Xi'an and has been added to the World Heritage List. It was built in 652 during the Tang Dynasty (618-907) to store the Buddhist scriptures and statues that the great monk Xuanzang brought back from ancient India. The pagoda was rebuilt in 704 during the reign of Empress Wu Zetian, and its exterior brick façade was renovated during the Ming Dynasty. The Big Wild Goose Pagoda is almost a must-visit for tourists in Xi'an, as it is the largest and earliest Tang Dynasty square pavilion-style brick pagoda in existence. Its majestic tower features a pavilion-style design. There are two Goose Pagodas in Xi'an: the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda and the Small Wild Goose Pagoda. Both were built during the Tang Dynasty but have different histories. The Big Wild Goose Pagoda was completed earlier, while the Small Wild Goose Pagoda was built slightly later. This difference in timing may be attributed to various factors, such as changes in imperial priorities or shifts in religious patronage throughout the dynasty. They have different architectural styles. The original Small Wild Goose Pagoda was 15 stories high, but now only 13 stories remain, standing at 43.4 meters tall. It is a multi-eaved brick pagoda, while the Big Wild Goose Pagoda is 7 stories high and 64 meters tall, featuring a pavilion-style design. The Big Wild Goose Pagoda has straight lines that make it look tall and sturdy. In contrast, the Small Wild Goose Pagoda has a slightly curved shape that gives it a more graceful feel. Despite the differences, they both symbolized imperial support for Buddhism and the integration of religious practices into the state's cultural framework and are notable examples of such architectural marvels. Which is More Recommended to Visit Our guests visiting the Big Wild Goose Pagoda in autumn If you are short on time and can only visit one place, I recommend going to the Big Wild Goose Pagoda. It is climbable, allowing you to see some ancient artifacts, including original Buddhist inscriptions inside. The construction of the Big Wild Goose Pagoda involved intricate engineering techniques and artistic craftsmanship. It is not only a functional building but also represents the pinnacle of architectural design during the Tang era. The materials used, structural integrity, and decorative elements all contribute to its enduring legacy. Even today, this pagoda stands as a testament to the advanced building skills and deep spiritual beliefs of the people of the Tang Dynasty. You can observe the detailed carvings and inscriptions that adorn the pagoda, providing insights into the religious practices and daily life of the time. The pagoda also serves as an important landmark, attracting scholars, pilgrims, and tourists who come to admire its historical and cultural significance. If you have enough time, the Small Wild Goose Pagoda is also a good choice. It is located near the Xi'an Museum, allowing you to visit both sites in one trip. Within the Da Ci'en Temple, you can explore historic buildings such as the Great Hall of Buddha, the Bell Tower, and the Drum Tower. These ancient tablets showcase the art of Chinese calligraphy and highlight the contributions of the esteemed monks to the temple and Buddhism. When visiting the Big Wild Goose Pagoda, climbing the tower is a must; otherwise, you'll miss many classic cultural relics. • First Floor: On the south gate of the first floor, you'll find numerous inscribed tablets bearing the names of those who passed the imperial examination. • Second Floor: A statue of Sakyamuni Buddha is located here. • Third Floor: The birch bark scriptures brought back by Xuanzang are displayed on this floor. • Fourth Floor: This floor houses important relics. • Fifth Floor: You can see Tang Dynasty lotus-patterned square bricks and wind chimes. • Sixth and Seventh Floors: These floors serve as observation decks, offering views of the musical fountain and the Ever-bright Tang Mall. For the best photographs, position yourself at the south gate with Xuanzang's statue, or capture the Buddha statue in the Da Ci'en Temple Ruins Park next door. If you are in good health, I highly recommend climbing the tower. There is an additional charge of 25 yuan (3-4 USD) per person for the climb. Inside, you'll navigate a wooden staircase with 256 steps, and the visit takes about 20 minutes. Best Times to Visit the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda Big Wild Goose Pagoda taken from the northeast entrance of the Grand Tang Dynasty Ever Bright City The ideal times to visit the Big Wild Goose Pagoda are in spring (April to May) and late autumn (November). During spring, the temple's peony garden bursts into bloom, showcasing a stunning array of peonies in various colors. In late autumn, the persimmon trees are laden with ripe fruit, creating a unique and picturesque scene. For the best experience, visit in the morning (8:30 am-9 pm) or in the afternoon (4 pm-4:30 pm). In the morning, if you're lucky, you might catch monks reciting sutras in the temple. While the monks' chanting schedule isn't fixed, it typically occurs shortly after the temple opens, when there are fewer tourists and less distraction. After your visit to the pagoda, consider taking a stroll around the nearby the Grand Tang Dynasty Ever Bright City in the evening. How to Get to the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda The Big Wild Goose Pagoda is situated in a bustling area, and traffic can become quite congested during holidays and weekends. Since it is a bit far from the nearest subway station, traveling by private car is recommended. This way, you can be dropped off close to the pagoda and easily picked up after your visit. If you prefer to take the subway, you can exit at Big Wild Goose Pagoda Station on Line 3. The Giant Wild Goose Pagoda was constructed during the Tang Dynasty to facilitate the study and translation of Buddhist scriptures. Over the centuries, the pagoda has endured significant damage from weather, wars, and seismic activity, which has led to the loss of much of its original materials. Despite this, the pagoda remains a prominent structure of its style and significance. The Tang government commissioned the construction of the pagoda to house a chamber for translating Buddhist texts, in hopes of persuading the renowned monk Xuanzang to lead the temple. Xuanzang was a pivotal figure in Buddhism; he traveled to India to study and translate Sanskrit scriptures, and he developed influential theories on consciousness, karma, and rebirth that were later adopted by various schools of Buddhism.
